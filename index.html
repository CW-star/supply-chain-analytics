Key Findings

What the Data Reveals

Four critical insights with specific, quantified, actionable levers.

⚠ Shipping Crisis
95.3%

First Class has a 95.3% late delivery rate across every market. Africa: 97%. Europe: 95.8%. Yet it remains profitable — a hidden long-term trust timebomb invisible in the P&L.

📈 Demand Shock
Oct 2017

Prophet expected 5,355 orders. Actual: 2,255 — a 56.5% collapse. Without the model this looks like gradual decline. With it, you see an exact event boundary and cause to investigate.

📈 Revenue Gap
$1.6M

Across four post-shock months, $1,614,835 in expected revenue was lost. January 2018 revenue was 68% below the pre-shock average. Modeled, month-by-month, and quantified.

💻 Structural Loss
Computers

9 of 10 worst orders are Computers — losses of −$2,255 to −$3,443 per order at 0–12% discounts. Pricing problem, not a promotions problem.

💡 My Insights
What I Learned & What the Data Told Me
  • 1
    What I set out to answer
    Give supply chain leadership one complete picture: where deliveries fail, what caused the 2017 collapse, which products are structurally unprofitable — with a dollar amount on each finding.
  • 2
    What surprised me
    The October 2017 demand shock was invisible in raw data. Only the Prophet forecast overlay revealed it. Without the model you see decline. With it, you see a $1.6M hole with an exact start date.
  • 3
    The uncomfortable finding
    First Class generates profit despite failing 95% of customers. No short-term financial pressure to fix it. The right framing is a customer lifetime value model that makes the long-term cost visible to the CFO.
  • 4
    What I would do next
    Connect to live order data, build a real-time late delivery alert at 48 hours past SLA, and add a CLV model to convert the 95% late rate into a dollar-denominated risk for the finance team.

What the Data Reveals

Four actionable insights — each pointing to a specific intervention the business can make today.

🔴 Contract Risk
42.71%

Month-to-month churn at 42.71% — 15x higher than Two-year at 2.83%. Signing a one-year contract drops churn probability from 43% to 11% instantly.

Key Findings

What the Segmentation Reveals

Four segments with dramatically different revenue profiles and retention needs.

★ Champions
73.7%

Champions are just 20.2% of customers but generate $13.1M — 73.7% of all revenue. Average spend: $11,014. Last purchase: 27 days ago. Losing one Champion costs more than acquiring 13 new customers. These must be protected at all cost.

⚠ At-Risk
228 days

1,465 customers who used to buy regularly are now 228 days silent. They represent $2.9M in annual revenue that is actively slipping away. These customers already know the brand — a targeted reactivation campaign has the highest ROI of any marketing action.

📈 New / Promising
$865

1,251 recent customers averaging just $865 — but they bought 28 days ago, the same recency as Champions. The difference is frequency. The goal: convert these into Champions through onboarding, product education, and the first upsell before they go cold.

💔 Lost / Inactive
396 days

1,974 customers — 33.6% of the base — last purchased 396 days ago and spend only $326 on average. They generate 3.6% of revenue. A last-chance reactivation campaign makes sense; if there is no response, reallocating that marketing spend to Champions and At-Risk yields 10x better ROI.

My Insights
💡 My Insights from This Project
What I Learned &
What the Model Told Me
  • 1
    Why RFM over pure ML
    K-Means on raw RFM values produced mathematically clean clusters that map directly to business actions. This is more valuable than a black-box model with higher accuracy — a marketing team can understand "Champion" and act on it immediately.
  • 2
    The 73.7% finding changes the budget conversation
    Before this analysis, marketing spend was presumably distributed somewhat evenly across the customer base. After it, the conversation shifts: 73.7% of revenue is concentrated in 20% of customers. Protecting those 1,188 Champions should absorb a disproportionate share of the retention budget.
  • 3
    New vs Promising is the hidden opportunity
    The New/Promising segment bought just 28 days ago — the same recency as Champions. The only difference is frequency. This means the onboarding window is still open. A structured 60-day email sequence targeting this segment has an outsized expected return.
  • 4
    What I would do next
    Add a predictive churn probability score to each customer using logistic regression, connect to a CRM via API, and build a live dashboard that flags when a Champion's recency score starts degrading — before they become At-Risk.

Key Findings

What the Data Reveals

Five findings that reframe how Chicago's transit investment should be prioritized.

📈 Correlation
r = 0.92

Income and transit access have a near-perfect 0.92 Pearson correlation across Chicago's 40 community areas. This is not a coincidence — it is a structural pattern baked into decades of infrastructure investment decisions that systematically favored wealthier neighborhoods.

⚠ Access Gap
4x Gap

High income areas average 15 CTA stops within 0.5 miles. Low income areas average just 3.6 stops. That is a 4x transit advantage for wealthy neighborhoods — and the gap is widest in the communities where car ownership is lowest and transit dependence is highest.

💔 Worst Cases
2 stops

Fuller Park, Englewood, and West Englewood each have only 2 CTA stops, median incomes of $20-24K, and 44-52% of households without a vehicle. These residents depend entirely on transit — and have almost none. Any new service investment should start here.

🌐 Geographic Split
11.8 vs 4

North/Central Chicago averages 11.8 stops per community area. The West Side and South Side average 4.4 and 4.0 — less than one-third. This is not a density or demand problem. Both the South Side and West Side have high population density and high no-vehicle rates.

My Insights
💡 My Insights from This Project
What the Analysis
Told Me About Urban Equity
  • 1
    Why this matters beyond transit
    Transit access is a proxy for economic opportunity. A resident in Fuller Park with no car and 2 nearby CTA stops has fundamentally fewer options for employment, healthcare, and education than someone in Lincoln Park with 15 stops. The transit gap IS an opportunity gap.
  • 2
    The 0.92 correlation is the headline
    A correlation of 0.92 in social science data is remarkable. It means income alone explains 85% of the variance in transit access across Chicago neighborhoods. This gives policymakers a clear, defensible case for equity-based investment criteria.
  • 3
    My urban design background shaped this analysis
    Understanding how cities are structured — zoning, density, street networks, historical disinvestment patterns — helped me ask better questions of the data. The West Side and South Side transit gaps are not random. They reflect specific policy decisions made over decades.
  • 4
    What I would do next
    Add actual route-level frequency data (not just stop counts), layer in employment center locations to model actual commute feasibility, and build a live Tableau map where city planners can explore the equity data interactively by neighborhood.
